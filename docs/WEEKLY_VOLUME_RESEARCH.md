# Weekly resistance-training volume evidence review

Research checked on 12 November 2026. Prepared for GymLog idea 5. This is a focused review of the major relevant syntheses and informative trials, with a targeted search for age, sex, training history and fractional set counting. It is not a registered systematic review or an individualized training prescription.

## Findings that should determine the feature

The defensible product is a weekly set-volume estimate with editable goals and transparent reference information. The literature supports a dose-response association and diminishing returns. It does not supply a validated function that converts a person's age, gender and years of lifting into their optimal weekly sets for every muscle. A single exact number labelled “optimum” would overstate the evidence. The studies and distinctions below explain this judgment.

### Weekly sets and the meaning of a reference range

- **Current dose-response synthesis.** Pelland et al., published online in December 2025 and in the February 2026 issue of *Sports Medicine*, analysed 67 studies with 2,058 participants. Participants were predominantly male, with an average age near 25. Direct sets counted as 1 and indirect sets as 0.5 fitted the data better than counting indirect work fully or excluding it. More weekly volume was associated with greater hypertrophy, with diminishing returns. This supports an approximate counting convention, not an experimentally calibrated coefficient for every movement or an individually optimal upper limit. The short interventions and young sample limit transport to other populations. [Paper and abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41343037/), [authors' data and materials](https://osf.io/6z3xu)

- **Current ACSM synthesis.** The 2026 position stand combined 137 systematic reviews covering more than 30,000 participants. Its evidence search was current to October 2024. Higher volume, including at least 10 weekly sets, enhanced hypertrophy. This is a population-level finding from heterogeneous programmes. It should not become a rule that everyone needs exactly 10, or that a week below 10 is ineffective. The review separately treats strength, hypertrophy and power, which require distinct interpretations. [ACSM position stand](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41843416/)

- **Earlier dose-response evidence.** Schoenfeld et al. included 15 studies and 34 treatment groups. Their meta-regression associated each additional weekly set with an effect-size increment of 0.023, approximately 0.37 percentage points of additional hypertrophy over the intervention. Their categories were below 5, 5–9 and at least 10 sets. This association is not a prediction of how much muscle one person will gain from an extra set. [2017 meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27433992/)

- **Why “12–20” is a useful context band with limits.** Baz-Valle et al.'s 2022 review included seven studies of trained young adults, with six entering the quantitative comparison. They compared 12–20 weekly sets with more than 20. Pooled differences were not significant for quadriceps or biceps, with a signal favouring higher volume for triceps. Outcomes, regions and counting practices varied. Its eligibility criteria included age 18–35 and at least a year of training, so this band is not an established target for children, older adults or every beginner. Older raw-set conventions also cannot be assumed identical to the newer fractional count. [Review and inclusion criteria](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8884877/)

### Training history and individual responses

- **A trial finding greater growth with higher volume.** Schoenfeld et al. randomized resistance-trained men to 1, 3 or 5 sets per exercise per session, three times weekly for eight weeks. Thirty-four completed the intervention. Higher volume produced greater growth at several measured sites, while strength gains did not differ between groups. Counting all compound sets produced weekly totals as high as 30 upper-body and 45 lower-body sets. Those totals should not be transferred into a fractional-set goal without recoding. [Randomized trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30153194/)

- **A different trained cohort showed no advantage from more.** Aube et al. compared 12, 18 and 24 lower-body weekly sets in 35 trained participants over eight weeks. Muscle thickness and regional lean-mass changes did not show a volume-group advantage. The contrast with other trials illustrates why one universal saturation threshold is not established. [Randomized trial](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000003524)

- **Previous volume can matter, but a universal progression percentage is unsupported.** Scarpelli et al. used a within-person study in 16 trained participants, comparing one leg at a fixed 22 sets per week with the other at 120% of the participant's previous logged volume for eight weeks. The individualized approach produced a greater vastus-lateralis cross-sectional-area change, with a mean difference of 1.08 cm² and a 95% confidence interval of 0.04–2.11. This small muscle-specific study supports considering training history, not prescribing 20% more to everyone. [Primary trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32108724/)

- **Maintenance can also work.** Barsuhn et al. randomized 55 trained men to maintain prior volume or increase it by 30% or 60%. Twenty-nine completed eight weeks. All groups improved muscle measures, with no between-group difference in muscle-size change. Attrition and the small final sample constrain confidence, but the result provides a direct counterexample to an algorithm that continually raises targets merely because experience or training time increases. [Primary trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39665246/)

### Sex, gender and age

- **Sex does not justify an automatic set multiplier.** Refalo et al.'s 2025 Bayesian meta-analysis compared males and females receiving the same interventions. Twenty-nine studies were analysed, mostly in untrained young adults. Relative muscle growth was similar between sexes, with an estimated between-sex difference of 0.69% and a 95% highest-density interval from −1.50% to 2.88%. Absolute changes modestly favoured males. Only six studies involved participants with training experience. These findings do not identify sex-specific optimal set counts. Gender identity was not the exposure studied, so collecting “gender” cannot justify a biological-volume adjustment. [Sex-differences meta-analysis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11869894/)

- **Trained females can respond to different volumes.** Enes et al. studied 30 females with approximately 2.1 years of training experience over 12 weeks. Constant 22 weekly sets were compared with fortnightly increases of two or four sets. The larger progression improved one cross-sectional-area outcome versus constant volume, while summed muscle-thickness changes did not differ. This is useful female-specific evidence, but it does not establish a female optimum or a numerical ratio against males. [Primary trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39869076/)

- **Older-adult hypertrophy evidence is less precise for dose.** Santana et al. reviewed 32 randomized studies in adults at least 65 years old, with 28 in meta-analysis. Resistance training improved whole-muscle size and fibre area. Weekly sets and age were not significant moderators of the examined hypertrophy outcomes. That result means this dataset did not establish a reliable relationship, not that volume and age have no effect. [Older-adult meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39579806/)

- **Single versus multiple sets gives a mixed outcome picture in older groups.** Marques et al. included 15 studies and 430 participants aged at least 50, of whom 93% were women. Multiple sets modestly favoured lower-limb strength and muscle quality. The pooled muscle-size contrast was not significant, SMD 0.15 with 95% CI −0.07 to 0.37. These data do not validate a fixed percentage reduction in hypertrophy volume after age 50 or 65. [Older-group volume review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36307745/)

- **Exclude retracted threshold evidence.** The 2019 paper “Evidence for an Upper Threshold for Resistance Training Volume in Trained Women” was retracted in 2021. Its low-volume ceiling must not be used to establish recommendations. [Retraction notice](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33986236/)

### Per-exercise muscle coefficients

The per-exercise registry is a model of muscle exposure. Direct and indirect roles need
individual review before use. An arbitrary 0.7 triceps weight would imply precision the
audit has not established. The broad convention in the first source summary does not
validate every metadata-derived exercise profile.

- **Electromyography percentages are unsuitable conversion factors.** Vigotsky et al. explain why acute surface-EMG amplitude is not a validated predictor of later hypertrophy. Normalizing an exercise's EMG signal to a maximum contraction does not yield a fraction of a hypertrophy-effective set. [Methodological paper](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35006527/)

- **Participation and growth are different.** Kubo et al. randomized 17 men to full- or half-squat training for ten weeks. Knee-extensor volume increased, gluteal and adductor growth varied by squat depth, and measured hamstring volume did not change. This cautions against awarding generic “leg” exercises full hamstring credit simply because hamstrings participate in the movement. [Primary muscle-volume trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31230110/)

## Product grouping proposal

The following categories organize onboarding and evidence explanations. They are product choices, not validated cut points in a biological dose-response function.

| Input | Proposed grouping | Intended use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Age | Under 18 | Tracking and user/coach goals only, with no adult-evidence optimum calculation |
| Age | 18–39 | Young-adult evidence context |
| Age | 40–64 | Evidence context explaining limited direct transfer from young cohorts |
| Age | 65+ | Older-adult evidence context |
| Training experience | Under 1 year of consistent resistance training | Beginner context, without an automatic low-set optimum |
| Training experience | 1 to under 3 years | Trained context |
| Training experience | At least 3 years | Longer-trained context, without automatically increasing targets |
| Training interruption | Returning after a break | Retain experience history but allow the user to set a new goal |
| Gender | Optional, inclusive identity field if another feature needs it | No volume multiplier |

Continuous age and months of training may be stored if needed for future evidence updates. Groups must be derived from those values so a future change does not require asking the person again. No date of birth is needed for this feature if an age band meets the requirement. Do not infer pregnancy, menopause, sex assigned at birth, hormone use, disability or rehabilitation needs from age or identity.

## Counting and comparison contract for a first implementation

These are proposed engineering rules. They make the estimate reproducible and auditable rather than claiming a newly discovered individualized optimum.

1. Count completed, non-skipped work sets in saved sessions within the selected local-calendar week. Include extra sets. Exclude warm-up/cool-down text and unsaved plans. A superset does not multiply set credit.
2. Each included set contributes its exercise coefficient separately to each muscle. The coefficients do not have to sum to 1, because one compound set can expose several muscles.
3. Keep direct sets and estimated indirect contribution visible separately. Let the user inspect the exercise breakdown. Call the total “estimated weekly sets”.
4. Version the mapping and record its source, rationale, review state and effective date. Historical results should either use a defined version or visibly state when recalculation changes the estimate.
5. Exclude private/custom exercises from the estimate until they have a reviewed mapping, as explicitly requested. Display the number of excluded completed sets so missing coverage is visible.
6. Treat static timed holds, unusual rehabilitation movements, lifts with ambiguous names and explosive/technical lifts as separately reviewed cases. Do not manufacture repetition-set equivalence from seconds, kilograms or name matching.
7. Permit user/coach weekly goals per muscle and retain their source as “your goal” or “coach goal”. Provide research context alongside them. Do not label a manually chosen goal as a scientifically calculated optimum.
8. A future automatic starting range needs a documented goal-specific policy and review. Hypertrophy, maximal strength, rehabilitation and general health must not share a single optimum formula. The evidence above can support educational population ranges, but their counting conventions must match the app's metric before comparison.

## Review gates before presenting a full-catalogue estimate

- Every selectable exercise needs either a reviewed mapping or an explicit exclusion reason. Completeness is not permission to guess.
- An anatomical reference can justify primary and secondary muscle roles, while longitudinal trials can refine specific cases. Store those different evidence types distinctly.
- A qualified reviewer should resolve mappings that depend on technique, grip, range of motion or equipment. For example, an unspecified chest-press machine cannot justify a universally precise triceps contribution.
- Verify unit tests with compound sets, unilateral work, extra/skipped sets, session corrections, custom exclusions, week boundaries and user isolation.
- Avoid a red “too little/too much” judgment based solely on population averages. A comparison should identify which goal it uses and show coverage and uncertainty.

## Search record and limits

Targeted queries covered weekly sets, hypertrophy dose-response, trained participants, sex differences, older adults, individualized previous volume, direct/indirect set accounting and EMG validity. Primary journal papers and author-authored syntheses were preferred. The main high-level evidence includes studies published through 2026, while some syntheses searched only through earlier dates. Search snippets were checked against PubMed or the primary paper where accessible. No exhaustive trial-level extraction or formal risk-of-bias re-analysis was performed. A coefficient for every exercise is a separate catalogue audit, not an output the scientific literature can directly supply.
